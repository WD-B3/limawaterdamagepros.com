How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work in Lima, OH

A scope of work is the plan on paper. Learn how to read it line by line.

By the Lima Water Damage Pros team · 2026

A scope of work is the document that lists what a restoration company will do, where and with what. It can look dense, but it follows a pattern. This guide walks through a typical scope so you can read yours before you approve it.

You are allowed to ask questions about any line. A scope you do not understand is not a scope you should sign.

The Header

The top of a scope lists the property address, the date of loss, the cause and the water category. Check that these are correct, since the category affects what can be dried.

The header may also list the insurer, claim number and adjuster if a claim is open.

Room-By-Room Line Items

Abbreviation or termWhat it meansWhy it matters
SFSquare feetSets the area for pricing
LFLinear feetUsed for baseboards and trim
Cat 1, 2, 3Water categorySets safety steps and what is removed
R&RRemove and replaceMaterial is thrown out and rebuilt
D&RDetach and resetMaterial is saved and reinstalled
DemoDemolitionRemoval of damaged material

A good scope defines its abbreviations or uses plain words. If not, ask.

The Equipment Log

The equipment log lists each air mover, dehumidifier and other device by room and date. It should match what you see on site.

Compare it with the daily readings. If the readings are dry and the equipment stays, ask why. The structural drying page explains how equipment is removed.

Demolition Versus Drying

Some materials are dried in place, and others are removed. The scope should say which and why. Carpet pad, insulation and soft drywall are common removals.

If a line says remove and replace without a reason, ask for the reading or the category behind it. The carpet water damage restoration page explains why some carpet stays and some goes.

Questions To Ask Before You Approve

  1. Which items are mitigation and which are repair?
  2. What are the readings for each material you plan to remove?
  3. What happens if hidden damage is found?
  4. Will the scope be updated and shared as work proceeds?
  5. What is excluded?

After The Work

At the end, ask for a final scope, the daily readings and photographs. These are the documents your insurer will want.

Keep them with your policy and your claim notes. They also help if you sell the house later.

Spotting Gaps In A Scope

A scope can leave things out as easily as put things in. Check that every wet room has a line, that equipment is listed for each, and that monitoring is included.

Look for work that will obviously be needed but is not listed, such as baseboard removal before drywall repair or contents handling before demolition.

If something is missing, ask whether it is excluded, included elsewhere or simply forgotten. Get the answer in writing and have the scope updated.

Using The Scope During The Job

Keep a copy where you can check it. As work proceeds, compare what you see with what is written, and ask about differences early.

If the crew adds or removes work, ask for an updated scope. A short email confirming the change is enough in many cases.

At the end, check that every line was completed or credited. The final scope and readings become part of your records.

A Walk-Through Example

Imagine a kitchen leak. The header lists the address, the date of loss, the source and the water category. The first room lists extraction by square foot and the equipment placed.

The next lines list the cabinet toe-kick removal, the wet flooring removed and the drywall cut out above the wet line. Each line has a unit, a quantity and a price.

After mitigation, the repair section lists new drywall, texture, paint, trim and flooring. These lines are often priced after readings show what remained wet.

The last section lists exclusions, such as mold testing or contents cleaning, and notes for hidden damage. Reading from top to bottom, you can see the story of the job.

Final Tips Before You Approve

Read the scope once for the story and once for the numbers. The story tells you what happened and what will be done, and the numbers tell you what it will cost.

If a line does not make sense, circle it and ask. Most companies are happy to explain, and the answer often teaches you something useful about your own house.

Approve only when you can describe the plan to someone else in a few sentences.

Share the scope with a trusted friend or a family member who has been through a claim. A second set of eyes often catches an item you glossed over, and it costs nothing.

Scopes For Lima Losses

A scope for a Lima basement after a heavy rain looks different from one for an upstairs bathroom leak. Basements add concrete drying and often contaminated water, while upstairs losses add ceilings below.

Our pages for Delphos and Ada describe local situations that change what a scope contains.

Questions About This Topic

Who writes the scope?

The restoration company writes it, and your insurer may write its own. Compare the two.

Can the scope change?

Yes, if hidden damage is found or readings change. A good company updates it in writing.

What does R&R mean?

Remove and replace.

Do I have to approve every line?

You approve the plan. Ask about any line you do not understand.

Should the scope list prices?

Usually yes, either by line or in total. Ask for line detail.
